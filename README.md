timeframe, in its author's window and in years the author never saw.

## Two numbers, and the order they were decided in

Read the last table again. Under the rules **declared before the sweep**, 66
strategies survive and **four beat the market**. Under the full rule set, six
survive and **none** does.

Both are published because the difference is not a better measurement — it is
the same measurement under a rule that was chosen later. Both bias detectors ran
on every strategy from the first commit and both were named in the pre-sweep
checklist; what was *not* fixed in advance was which of them disqualifies a
strategy. That was settled after seeing that the four beating the market
reported returns of **+63,645,298%** and **+29,748,386%**.

That is a degree of freedom left open in the pre-registration and closed after
the data — smaller than inventing a detector, and still real. It is spelled out,
with dates from `git log`, in **[LEDGER.md](LEDGER.md)**.

## Where the corpus actually dies

Not at the bias detectors. The largest single drop is `G2_is_pos`: **456 → 158**,
strategies that lose money in the window their own author chose. Two thirds of
published strategies are already negative before anyone tests them on new data.

The second largest is `G7_recursive` — indicator values that change with how
much history you feed them. Of the 51 killed there, **50 were measured drift**;
only one was the formality of an undeclared warm-up.

- **[BASELINE.md](BASELINE.md)** — why `Market change`, the line freqtrade
  prints for free under every backtest, decides the question.
- **[TRAPS.md](TRAPS.md)** — checks built from the freqtrade community's own
  [backtesting-traps](https://brookmiles.github.io/freqtrade-stuff/2021/04/12/backtesting-traps/)
  document, after a member pointed out that bias detectors are not enough.
  Nothing in that file is my idea, including the thresholds.
- **[corpus/INDEX.md](corpus/INDEX.md)** — one card per strategy, including
  every strategy that failed to load, with the exception that killed it.
  Nothing is omitted for being inconvenient.
- **[LEDGER.csv](LEDGER.csv)** — the whole thing as one table, if you would
  rather check it than read about it.

## The corpus is biased, and that is the point

Who publishes a strategy on GitHub? Someone whose in-sample curve looked good.
Nobody uploads the version that lost money in backtest. **This corpus is
selected by its own authors for best in-sample result** — the survivorship
filter runs before we ever see the file.

That is not a weakness in the sample; it is what makes the number mean
something. The question it answers is not "how do random strategies perform" but:

> Of the strategies people were confident enough to publish, how many hold up
> when someone re-runs them on data the author never saw?

A low survival rate in a sample pre-filtered for good results is a much stronger
statement than the same rate in a random sample would be.

Related: **65% of the 2,567 strategy classes found are copies** of a few
originals, propagated without anyone re-testing them. Seventeen of the 53
repositories contributed no original strategy at all — one of them holds 477
classes. Details in [CORPUS.md](CORPUS.md).

# A worked example: five strategies, in detail

The corpus answers *how many*. It does not show *how* a strategy fails. For
that, five strategies from
[`paulcpk/freqtrade-strategies-that-work`](https://github.com/paulcpk/freqtrade-strategies-that-work)
were audited line by line.

```
                             avg trade %     p     avg trade %     p      survives
                              (in-sample)             (out)
EMAPriceCrossoverWithThreshold   1.39      0.113       0.65      0.161       47%
DoubleEMACrossoverWithTrend      0.38      0.049       0.19      0.290       50%
MACDCrossoverWithTrend           0.42      0.128       0.05      0.883       12%
RSIDirectionalWithTrendSlow      1.03      0.381       0.27      0.924       26%
RSIDirectionalWithTrend          0.34      0.238      -0.07      0.556    negative
```

In-sample window is the authors': 2018-03-01 … 2020-03-01, 1h, 8 USDT pairs.
Out-of-sample: 2020-03-01 … 2026-08-20, same code, same pairs, 0.1% fee per side.

**Four of the five were never statistically significant in their author's own
window, and none is significant out of sample.** Only
`DoubleEMACrossoverWithTrend` clears p < 0.05 in-sample, and only just (0.049).
The strongest performer of the set — `EMAPriceCrossoverWithThreshold` at 1.39%
per trade — sits at **p = 0.113**. For most of them the out-of-sample collapse
is beside the point: the in-sample result was never established.

**Three of the five have nothing left once execution is priced honestly.**
freqtrade fills at the candle open with no spread and no slippage; on XLM, DASH
and ADA in 2018–2020, hourly spreads of 0.3–0.5% were routine.

## Reproduce it

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
python fetch_bulk.py 1h   # hourly candles per pair, from Binance monthly archives
python harness.py         # the five case studies: both windows, both detectors
python ledger.py --csv    # one row per strategy, with provenance
```

About 4 minutes for the download, 3 for the five audits. The full corpus sweep
is `python corpus.py --shard 0/8` — eight processes, roughly 90 minutes on 12
cores. No API key: the data comes from Binance's public mirror, and nothing here
talks to an exchange account.

**Auditing your own strategy?** → **[CHECKLIST.md](CHECKLIST.md)**: thirteen
mechanical questions, each tied to a defect actually found here — eight in the
audited strategies, four in this pipeline.

## Method, and what it cannot do

The original `.py` files are run by **freqtrade 2026.7 itself** — same engine,
same execution semantics, no interpretation on my part. An earlier version
re-implemented the logic in pandas and produced 334 trades where freqtrade
produces 303; a re-implementation invites the fair reply *"you rewrote my logic
wrong"*, and the engine does not. That code is gone.

Both of freqtrade's own analysers run on every strategy — `lookahead-analysis`
and `recursive-analysis` — and both are reported whatever they say, including
when they clear the strategy.

Caveats, stated rather than omitted:

- `minimal_roi` is not applied where the published file comments it out. If the
  author's config carried one, the numbers move.
- Fee is a proxy for total round-trip cost. Fills are at the candle open with no
  spread and no slippage, so **every figure here is optimistic** — which is why
  the cost-sensitivity table exists.
- Trade counts differ from the authors' because `max_open_trades` is not
  published. That is a property of the repository, not of the measurement.
- **"Eight pairs" is the request list, not what traded throughout.** Three of
  the eight span the author's window; DASH was not listed on Binance until it
  was more than half over, and XMR was delisted on 2024-02-20. Measured with
  `coverage.py`, per pair, and recorded on every card.
- Coverage is measured at the edges of each window, not in the middle. A ragged
  series with intact edges would report 100%.

## What is deliberately not here

- **The price data.** 48 MB anyone can re-download. Publishing the fetch script
  is lighter and better science: you get the data yourself rather than trusting
  mine.
- **Any configuration, key, or host.** The audit touches no account and no
  server. `tools/secret_gate.py` refuses a commit containing a credential — with
  a self-test and a sabotage check that plants three real-shaped secrets and
  requires the gate to catch all three.

## Corrections

Nine errors in this audit have been found, published and fixed — including a
metric that was not scale-free, a claim about five strategies made from the one
I was looking at, and a consistency check that could not catch its own error
because both sides used the same wrong formula.

They are collected in **[CORRECTIONS.md](CORRECTIONS.md)**, with what each one
changed. An audit that hides its own corrections is asking for a trust it has
not earned.

## Get in touch

**📧 faxesuxan24@gmail.com** — for a private audit of a strategy you would
rather not post publicly. That is most of them, and it is a reasonable thing to
want.

- **Want your strategy audited the same way?** Open an issue with a link to the
  code — or run `harness.py` yourself. It is the exact pipeline used here, not a
  demonstration of one.
- **Found an error?** Open an issue. Errors found in this repository get
  published *in* it rather than quietly patched.

**What you get:** the thirteen checks in [CHECKLIST.md](CHECKLIST.md) run
against your code — both windows, p-value, buy-and-hold baseline, cost
sensitivity, both bias detectors — plus a written finding for anything that does
not hold.

**What you will not get:** a verdict on whether your idea is good. Every check
here is about whether your *measurement* can be trusted. A well-measured
strategy with no edge is still a strategy with no edge; you will simply learn it
sooner and cheaper.
